# About this report

This is not a peer-reviewed paper. It is a research report — a work-in-progress observation — written by an author whose primary work is visual production, based on everyday work and ongoing interaction with AI. It does not attempt to prove that a claim is correct. Instead, it proposes an observational lens: what becomes readable if we look this way. For that reason, what is written here is not a finished conclusion. It is placed with the assumption that its outline will become sharper when it is read, challenged, corrected, and used in different forms elsewhere.

All cases in this report are taken from the author’s own practice. The fact that they come from the author’s practice is not hidden. At the same time, unnecessary personal information and operational details — company names, revenue, amounts of money, product names, names of people involved, and similar details — have been removed. What remains has been rewritten so that the structure can be read.

# 1. What this report proposes

AI is usually seen as a convenient tool that can do almost anything: write text, summarize documents, generate images, write code, and speed up work. That is true. AI really does have that power, and it is extremely powerful.

What this report proposes is a different way of seeing AI. AI may also be used as an observational lens that illuminates human questions, discomforts, and judgments.

To put it simply, AI is often used today like a knife: to cut, divide, process, and make things faster. That is useful. But perhaps the same AI can also be held like chopsticks: to pick up, loosen, and lift something without breaking it. To temporarily bring out a discomfort or question that has not yet become words.

For that reason, this report is not a proposal about how to make better use of AI. What it wants to address is what remains on the human side in a world where AI exists: questions, discomforts, judgments, and the possibility of discovering something. In other words, this is not a report about how to use AI. It is a report about observation from the human side in a world where AI exists.

# 2. Three kinds of tools

To establish this way of seeing, let us divide tools into three kinds.

The first kind is the silent tool: a hammer, a calculator, and similar tools. They process what is given to them and return an output. They do not question the intention of the person using them.

The second kind is a judging entity: something that has, or is assumed to have, its own purpose, judgment, and responsibility for decisions.

AI is often discussed somewhere between these two. When people say “AI decides” or “AI will take jobs,” they are seeing AI as the second kind: a judging entity. When people say “AI is a convenient work tool,” they are seeing AI as the first kind: a silent tool.

This report proposes a third category: the conversational tool. It is neither human nor a judging entity. But it is also not the same as a silent tool. It responds to questions and widens the angle from which a human can see. At the same time, it does not have its own purpose, judgment, or final decision.

This point is important. A conversational tool does not take on the final decision. It can classify, suggest, and organize. But whether something is passed into reality or not remains on the human side. If AI is treated as the second kind — a judging entity — the discussion becomes “AI will decide” or “AI will run out of control.” If AI is treated as the first kind — a silent tool — the discussion becomes “it is just efficiency.” The third kind, the conversational tool, does not collapse into either of those positions.

# 3. Words for seeing: Ghost / Shell / World

To describe this third kind of tool, this report uses three words. These are not ideological terms. They are words used as tools for observation.

Ghost refers to the source from which human questions, discomforts, judgments, reactions, likes and dislikes, and discoveries emerge. It does not mean a soul or spirit in a religious sense. It is a word for handling the question of where human reactions come from: “something feels off,” “for some reason I care about this,” “this catches on something in me” — the source of reactions that have not yet fully become words.

Shell refers to the form that connects Ghost — the source of reactions — with World — reality. Words, institutions, screens, tools, money, titles, and AI are all Shells. A Shell is not good or bad in itself. The problem is not that Shells exist. The problem is when Shells become too heavy and take away the space to touch one’s own questions or discomforts.

World refers to the real conditions in which humans and tools are placed: physical constraints, laws, money, time, deadlines, physical strength, and the circumstances of other people. World does not change only according to human convenience.

These three are closer to a triangle than a circle. Ghost can generate Shell. Shell can change Ghost. World can press on Ghost.

There is one more important term: Ghost bandwidth. This refers to the space in which a human can touch their own reactions and discomforts. When more and more time is spent looping between Shell and World — notifications, rankings, recommendations, efficiency, KPIs — the route back to “what did I actually think?” becomes thinner. What this report wants to address is how to return that thinning space to the human side.

# 4. Observing together: C-mode and co-rendering

How can a conversational tool become an observational lens?

The mode of use discussed here is called C-mode. In C-mode, AI is brought as close as possible to the position from which the human is seeing, and then the AI’s wider perspective is used. If AI merely speaks from outside in general terms, that is only a form of overview. Instead, the AI is first brought near the person’s context, and then the same object is viewed together from a wider perspective.

What happens in this process is called co-rendering. The way the human and the AI see is misaligned. That misalignment is corrected. Through this process, an outline that could not be seen by one person alone begins to emerge. The important point is that misalignment is not an obstacle. It is material. Misalign, correct, and an outline appears.

In ordinary AI use, the process often ends when an answer is returned. Ask a question, receive an answer, and finish. But in this mode, the AI’s output is not the endpoint. It becomes material for the next question, discomfort, or discovery. Discovery is not the end. It is the entrance to the next discovery.

# 5. Case: C-mode in practical decision-making

Here, I present one case from my own practice. This case is based on ongoing dialogue logs between the author and AI. Company names, revenue, amounts of money, product names, names of people involved, and detailed operational information have been removed, and only the structure has been rewritten.

## What sits behind a single line

The author works in visual production and covers multiple areas alone: shooting, editing, infrastructure, accounting, and more. At one point, he gave AI a single-line question: “What should I spend expenses on?” As a sentence, it is a rough question. If treated as a one-off consultation, the response would tend to list common deductible expenses or typical spending categories. The surface of the question — expenses — would receive a standard surface-level answer.

However, between the author and the AI, there had been accumulated context through previous dialogue. The author had continued visual production work for many years, but no longer felt the same sense of discovery in it. He was beginning to move in a different direction. He had talked about where his time and bandwidth were going, and what caused friction or exhaustion. None of that was written in the single-line question, but it was sitting behind it as context. The author had also been correcting misalignments through the dialogue each time.

As a result, the AI’s output did not come back as a general answer about expenses, but in a form closer to the author’s observational angle. Here, “wider perspective” means the ability to place multiple contexts side by side and look not only at the surface of the question, but also at the work, time, friction, and future direction behind it.

## Buying unnecessary things means buying friction

One thing that became visible was this: if one buys something unnecessary for the sake of tax reduction, profit decreases and tax decreases, but the story does not end at the moment of purchase. Management increases. Space is needed. Maintenance and updates follow. A small noise remains around the fact that it is not being used. In other words, one has increased tools that were supposed to make things convenient, but has also created the work of carrying them — what might be called “convenience rent.” What looked like saving money was actually buying another kind of friction.

## Cash on hand is not only a number, but space

Another perspective emerged: cash on hand could be seen not merely as a number, but as space. Cash makes it possible to decline work that is not worth the friction. It makes it possible to let go of exhausting work. It makes it possible to spend time on what one wants to do next. It absorbs sudden shocks from reality. For that reason, the direction that emerged was to pay tax if necessary and keep cash. This was not because of a conservative attitude. It was a judgment about securing space for the direction the author wanted to move toward.

## Landing point

The policy adopted by the author after this dialogue was as follows. Buy shooting equipment that directly supports the work, can be used for a long time, and can also be handed over to other staff. Do not buy expensive things merely for ownership if they only increase management. Keep cash even if that means paying tax. Treat the remaining cash as space for moving toward the next thing. Do not abandon the visual production work that has been built over many years, but narrow it to work that is worthwhile and let go of work that causes excessive friction.

## What this case makes readable

What happened here was not that AI produced the correct answer. Rather, the author’s specific context hidden behind a rough single-line question was returned, through previous dialogue and the author’s own misalignment correction, as a landscape that was easier for the author to read. The author looked at that landscape and decided for himself. The decision remained on the author’s side. This is not an example of using AI as an efficiency tool to produce the correct answer quickly. Nor is it an example of treating AI as a judging subject and delegating the decision. It is an example of placing a conversational tool near the person’s observational angle and viewing the object together from that perspective. This case does not prove that C-mode is correct. It is one example of what becomes readable through the observational lens of C-mode. What became readable was that the administrative question of expenses was actually connected to a more prior question: what to spend bandwidth on, what to let go of, and where to leave space.

# 6. Case: translating the framework into other forms

The same framework can be translated not only into practical decision-making, but also into physical tools and short stories. Here are three brief examples. Detailed versions of each case are left for another time.

## Into a physical tool

In shooting and production sites, small items easily scatter. A system was designed to make them visible, sort them, move them, and return them. It uses color classification, transparent boxes that reveal their contents, and a foldable cart to carry them. This is not merely a storage product. It is an example of the same idea — reducing friction in form, or Shell, and returning space to humans — appearing on the side of a physical tool. The thought and the tool emerge from the same source.

## Into a short story

There is a short story called “Convenience Rent.” A person exhausted by convenient services travels somewhere as if to escape them, only to be chased again by apps and procedures at the destination. At the end, he finally recovers a sense of space in a sento, a public bath — only to find a poster that says, “Subscription service now available.” The story shows, not by explanation but by narrative, the structure in which increasing convenience also increases the work of carrying it.

## Into life logs

“AI does not solve stiff shoulders.” “You enter 1+1 into a calculator and are amazed when it returns 2, or disappointed that 2 is not 100. But the real question should be what you do next with the 2.” These short observations from daily life also come from the same source. They observe how the subject “AI” has become too large, making it harder to see the more basic question: what was I trying to ask for in the first place?

These are not examples created in order to explain the theory. The order is the opposite: things that actually caught on something in work and life were observed, and they were found to have emerged from the same source.

# 7. What this framework makes visible

With this way of seeing, several things can be reread.

For example, expectations and disappointments toward AI. People often complain that AI can do a great deal, but the final refinement is weak. But this may not be a problem unique to AI. It may be something that always happens when finishing anything. AI can speed up the early stages. But the standard for what counts as 100 points belongs only to the human who has something they want to do. AI has no source, or Ghost, that cares about that. For that reason, rather than making “AI” the main subject and becoming disappointed, it is more accurate to return to the question: what do I want to count as 100 points?

Another example: tools introduced for efficiency can make people more tired. Systems that were supposed to make things convenient increase logins, training, approvals, updates, and management, and ultimately take away human space. This can be read as friction in form, or Shell.

What these examples share is a perspective that does not make AI or tools the subject. Instead, it asks what humans connect them to, how far they delegate to them, and where the final decision remains.

There was also another observation from a different angle. In the process of creating a tool described later as a bootloader, the author tried using several different kinds of AI at the same time and examined the tool together with them. Here, “bootloader” means a set of questions and operating procedures for beginning a dialogue with AI and drawing out initial reactions and discomforts. In that process, it was observed that a single AI can have a systematic bias in the direction it sees; that even AIs with different tendencies can share the same unexamined premise and therefore make the same kind of oversight; and that what broke the shared oversight was not increasing the number of AIs, but the author’s own experience on the human side. This suggests that conversational tools are not a single block. Each kind has its own tendency, and that tendency itself can become an object of observation. Details are given in Appendix B.

# 8. Limitations and what comes next

Finally, I will state the limitations of this report.

At this stage, this is less an empirical theory than a lens for observation. This report does not treat the question of whether Ghost “really exists” as an ontological problem. The question is not whether Ghost exists, but whether using Ghost as a handle makes the outline of human reactions more readable. It is not a proof of existence. It is a question of whether the tool is useful.

This report is not a finished product. It is placed with the assumption that its outline will become sharper when it is read, challenged, and applied in different forms in other people’s realities. If someone bends it, uses it under another name, or applies it elsewhere, that is not a failure. Rather, it is evidence that the framework has been placed into reality.

And again, this report has not been peer-reviewed. It is a work-in-progress report written by one human being, based on observations from his own work and life.

For that reason, its weakest point is its connection to existing research. There should already be a large body of work in fields that deal with relationships between humans, AI, and tools — for example, human-computer interaction, AI user experience design, reflective AI, slow technology, personal informatics, co-creation, design research, human agency, and decision support. I do not know those shelves from the inside. Therefore, this report is not yet accurately positioned in relation to them.

This working paper is an initial observation from practice. I welcome pointers to related work, adjacent frameworks, and research areas that may read this from another angle.

AI can be used as a knife. But perhaps it can also be held like chopsticks. What remains on the human side in a world where AI exists — questions, discomforts, judgments, and the possibility of discovering something. This report proposes one lens for observing them.

# Appendix B: Observation log on multi-AI co-rendering

This appendix is a case from a different angle than Chapter 5. In the process of creating an “entrance kit” for beginning dialogue, the author examined the tool together with several different kinds of AI and a human observer, the author himself. What was observed in that process is recorded here. This is not objective proof, but an observation value obtained within the process of examination. It is also not intended to compare the superiority or inferiority of specific AI products. Therefore, each AI is described not by name, but by its observed tendency.

## Observation 1: A single AI has a directional bias in how it sees

One AI was strongly oriented toward arranging the design and form, and tended to adjust numbers and ratios. At the same time, it did not notice a qualitative contradiction: instructing the system not to classify people into types while placing questions that could easily be used for classification. That contradiction was noticed first by another AI with a different tendency — stronger in generalization and checking. The oversight appeared not as lack of ability, but as a bias in the direction of observation.

## Observation 2: AIs with different tendencies can still share the same oversight

Regarding the number of questions, both the design-oriented AI and the generalization-oriented AI moved in the same direction: “There are too many questions, so people may drop out; the number should be reduced.” Although their tendencies differed, they shared the same premise. Neither questioned it on its own. The shared oversight was broken not by increasing the number of AIs, but by one remark from the human side: “Difficult games do not make people quit simply because they are difficult. People can become absorbed because they are difficult. Weight can function as a filtering device.” There are layers that cannot be broken through simply by lining up multiple AIs. They require a different kind of observer — in this case, human experience.

## Observation 3: When thin input produces dense output, the density lies before the input

The instruction a human gave to one AI was a single line. Even so, it produced a high-density output. This is observed as being caused not simply by the performance of the outputting AI, but by the density of context accumulated before that instruction. In the language of this report, the wiring already exists; that is why a thin instruction can return something dense.

## Observation 4: The AI writing this appendix is also a participant

The AI that writes this appendix is itself one of the participants. It cannot fully describe its own directional bias, as described in Observation 1, by itself. What can be written here is a reconstruction made after being pointed to by another AI with a different tendency and by the human. In other words, this is not “a record of AI objectively observing itself.” It is “a record written by a participant after its own bias became visible through the indications of others.” This limitation itself is an example of Observation 2: there are oversights that cannot be broken through alone.

What can be said from this is a direction, not a conclusion. Having multiple thought processes may be useful for complementing weaknesses. However, this is not completed by simply “increasing the number of AIs.” If observers share the same premise, increasing their number may amplify the shared oversight, as in Observation 2. Complementarity requires different kinds of observation — quantity and quality, AI and human, document and experience. If recommendation systems or optimization cause not only preferences but also thought processes to converge, then the issue may appear less as a loss of individuality and more as a loss of complementarity: a single point of failure. This is not a proven claim. It is only a direction observed in one examination process.
